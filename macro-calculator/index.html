THE BASICS

Why Count Macros Instead of Calories?

Calories tell you how much to eat. Macros tell you what to eat. A macro calculator breaks your daily intake into protein, carbs and fat — so you know your body is getting the right fuel, not just the right number. Whether your goal is weight loss, muscle gain, or maintenance, tracking macros gives you an actionable plan.

3 Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake #1: Only tracking calories. If you hit 1,800 calories but all of it came from pasta and bread, you're missing the protein your muscles need to recover. Calories control your weight. Macros control your body composition.

Mistake #2: Going too low on carbs. Carbs are not the enemy. They're your body's preferred fuel source, especially if you train 3+ times per week. Cutting carbs too aggressively leads to fatigue, poor workout performance, and rebound cravings.

Mistake #3: Treating all fats the same. A handful of almonds and a slice of pepperoni pizza might have similar fat grams, but your body processes them completely differently. Prioritize unsaturated fats from nuts, avocado, and olive oil — they support hormone health and vitamin absorption.

The bottom line: Counting macros isn't about restriction. It's about making sure every gram you eat is pulling in the same direction as your goal — whether that's losing fat, building muscle, or maintaining your current weight.

How This Calculator Works

Our calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, the most widely validated formula for estimating your basal metabolic rate (BMR) — the calories your body burns at rest. We then apply an activity multiplier based on your reported weekly training hours to get your total daily energy expenditure (TDEE).

Once we have your TDEE, we distribute it across three macronutrients using ranges recommended by the American College of Sports Medicine and the International Society of Sports Nutrition:

  • Protein: 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight — the sweet spot for muscle repair and satiety
  • Carbs: 3–5 g per kg, adjusted based on your training intensity — higher training volume needs more glycogen
  • Fat: 0.8–1.2 g per kg — enough to support hormone function without crowding out performance carbs

How these numbers map to your goals:

  • Fat loss: We bias toward higher protein (to preserve muscle in a deficit) and moderate carbs
  • Muscle gain: We increase carbs and total calories while keeping protein at the upper end
  • Maintenance: Balanced across all three, with slight adjustments based on training level

These are starting points. Adjust up or down by 10–15% after two weeks based on how your body responds. No calculator replaces listening to your own body.

More Than Just Numbers

What this looks like in real life:

Scenario A — The desk worker who trains 3× per week: Your plan might target 140 g protein, 250 g carbs, 65 g fat (~2,100 kcal). A sample day: Greek yogurt + granola for breakfast, chicken wrap for lunch, salmon + rice + roasted veggies for dinner, and a post-gym protein shake. Simple, repeatable, no weird ingredients.

Scenario B — The vegetarian trying to hit protein: Getting 130 g of protein without meat is absolutely doable. Focus on eggs, cottage cheese, Greek yogurt, tofu, tempeh, lentils, chickpeas, edamame, and a quality whey or plant-based protein powder. Combine grains and legumes in the same meal (rice + beans, hummus + whole wheat pita) for complete amino acid profiles.

Scenario C — The busy parent with no time to cook: Batch-prep 3–4 protein sources on Sunday (grilled chicken breast, hard-boiled eggs, baked tofu). Keep quick carb sources on hand (microwave rice pouches, whole wheat tortillas, oats). Add fats as toppings (avocado, olive oil drizzle, nut butter spoonful). You can assemble a macro-balanced meal in under 5 minutes without a recipe.

Where Do Our Food Suggestions Come From?

Most macro calculators stop at the numbers. We go one step further: once you know your daily targets, we show you familiar foods from your country's official government nutrition database to help you actually hit those targets.

We don't use generic food lists. Every serving suggestion in our calculator is sourced directly from:

  • 🇺🇸 United States — USDA FoodData Central, the U.S. Department of Agriculture's authoritative food composition database, covering over 400,000 foods with detailed macro and micronutrient profiles.
  • 🇨🇦 Canada — Canadian Nutrient File, Health Canada's standard reference database with nutrient data for foods commonly consumed in Canada.
  • 🇦🇺 Australia — Food Standards Australia New Zealand, the bi-national government agency responsible for food composition data across Australia and New Zealand.
  • 🇩🇪 Germany — Bundeslebensmittelschlüssel (BLS), Germany's federal food coding database, maintained by the Max Rubner-Institut.

Why this matters: Nutrition labeling standards vary by country. A “large egg” in the U.S. averages 50 g; in Australia it's 58 g. Protein content in chicken breast differs between USDA and Canadian Nutrient File due to farming practices. By using each country's own official data, we ensure the foods we recommend match what you'll actually see on your plate — and on your local nutrition labels.

More countries are being added. If yours isn't listed yet, the calculator still works — it defaults to USDA data, which is the most comprehensive global reference available.

FAQ

Questions, answered.

How much protein do I need per day?

Most adults need 1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, according to the International Society of Sports Nutrition. For a 70 kg (154 lb) person, that's 112–154 grams daily.

Why the range? Your exact number depends on your goal. If you're in a calorie deficit trying to lose fat, aim for the upper end (2.0–2.2 g/kg) — higher protein preserves muscle when you're eating less. If you're maintaining weight or building muscle, 1.6–2.0 g/kg is sufficient. Endurance athletes and older adults also benefit from the higher end to support recovery and prevent age-related muscle loss.

Spread your protein across 3–4 meals rather than one giant serving. Your body can only use so much at once for muscle protein synthesis.

How many carbs should I eat per day?

For most active adults, 3–5 grams of carbs per kilogram of body weight is a solid range. A 70 kg person would target 210–350 grams daily.

Carbs fuel your workouts and replenish glycogen stores. If you train 0–2 hours per week, stay near 3 g/kg. If you're doing 6+ hours of intense training (CrossFit, marathon prep, competitive sports), push toward 5 g/kg or higher.

Prioritize complex carbs: oats, brown rice, quinoa, whole wheat bread, potatoes, sweet potatoes, legumes, and fruit. These digest slower, keep you full longer, and provide steady energy — unlike simple sugars that spike and crash your blood sugar.

What are macros?

“Macros” is short for macronutrients — the three nutrients your body needs in large amounts: protein, carbohydrates, and fat. Each plays a distinct role:

  • Protein (4 kcal/g): Builds and repairs muscle, skin, hair, enzymes, and hormones. Found in meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, and tofu.
  • Carbohydrates (4 kcal/g): Your body's primary energy source. Found in grains, fruits, vegetables, and legumes.
  • Fat (9 kcal/g): Supports hormone production, vitamin absorption (A, D, E, K), and brain function. Found in oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, and fatty fish.

There's a fourth macronutrient — alcohol (7 kcal/g) — but it provides no nutritional value and isn't included in macro planning.

Counting macros simply means tracking how many grams of each you eat daily, rather than only counting total calories.
